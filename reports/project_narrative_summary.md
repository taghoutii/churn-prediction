# Churn Prediction — Project Narrative Summary

*A plain-language walkthrough of the reasoning connecting every step of this project, from raw data to final models. Written for understanding, not as a technical reference — see the notebooks and source code for implementation detail.*

## 1. The four raw datasets

The project started from four separate CSV exports, joined on a shared subscriber ID:

- **Socio-demographic data** — 423,767 rows, 7 columns. One row per subscriber: age, gender, marital status, preferred language, and contact city.
- **Churn/lifecycle data** — 423,767 rows, 18 columns. One row per subscriber: whether they churned, their tenure bucket, activation date, churn date (if applicable), first/last call and recharge dates, and a few administrative fields.
- **Monthly usage aggregates** — 2,668,258 rows, 24 columns. One row per subscriber per month: recharges, call minutes, SMS counts, data volume, and revenue.
- **Data bundle purchases** — 785,499 rows, 3 columns. One row per subscriber per month: spend on mobile data bundles specifically, merged in alongside the monthly usage data.

Across every date field in the combined data, the earliest recorded date is **December 26, 2002** (an account activation) and the latest is **March 19, 2025**. The monthly usage history itself is much narrower — it only covers **July 2024 through January 2025**, seven months — while the lifecycle and churn-event data extends a bit further, into March 2025.

## 2. What data cleaning fixed, and why each fix mattered

Several real data-quality issues surfaced during the initial audit, and each one shaped a later decision rather than just being a cosmetic fix.

Age contained implausible values (outside a 0–100 range). These were set to missing rather than guessed at or corrected — a choice that mattered later, because it preserved an honest record of *which* subscribers had bad age data, rather than quietly inventing a plausible-looking age for them. That distinction turned out to matter: missingness itself later proved to carry churn signal for several demographic fields, and an early decision to "fix" the age values rather than null them would have erased that signal before anyone had a chance to notice it.

The churn date field looked, at first glance, like a precise per-customer event timestamp. It isn't. A large share of churn dates cluster on a small number of exact administrative dates — the top 15 dates alone account for close to 30% of all recorded churners — which points to churn being processed in periodic batches rather than logged the instant a customer actually left. This finding directly shaped the eventual prediction design: rather than trying to predict an exact churn day (which the data can't support with that kind of precision), the project settled on a coarser, more robust target — whether a subscriber churns at all within a 90-day window — which absorbs the batch-processing noise instead of being distorted by it.

A minor but real issue: the customer-language field contained two spellings of French, "Francais" and "Français," which were silently splitting one real category into two. These were merged, a small fix, but one that matters for anything downstream that treats language as a categorical variable (both the demographic analysis and, eventually, the model).

Finally, a number of usage fields — data volume, data revenue, voice revenue — had missing values that, on inspection, meant "no activity that period," not "unknown." These were filled with zero rather than imputed, preserving the distinction between a customer who is dormant (a real, informative state) and one whose data is simply absent.

## 3. Feature engineering: what was built and why

The final feature set is built around a single fixed snapshot date, aggregating each subscriber's behavior over the 90 days before it. Every feature traces back to a specific finding from the exploratory or survival work, not a generic guess at what "might be useful":

- **Average recharge amount, recharge frequency, on-net call minutes, data volume, and data revenue** — the core usage-level features, included because the exploratory analysis found usage variables strongly right-skewed and behaviorally very different between churners and non-churners, and because the behavioral analysis showed usage collapsing sharply before churn.
- **Days since last call** — a recency feature that directly operationalizes the temporal-analysis finding that churners show much longer gaps in activity than non-churners, and the behavioral finding that activity falls to near-zero in roughly the final 90 days before churn.
- **Tenure in days** — kept as a continuous number rather than the raw bucketed tenure category, because tenure turned out, independently, to be the single strongest churn signal across the exploratory analysis, the Cramér's V association check, and the survival analysis (both the Kaplan-Meier curves and the Cox model).
- **Recency relative to tenure** — days-since-last-call divided by tenure, capturing that a month of silence means something very different for a two-month-old account than a three-year-old one; grounded in the same recency/tenure findings above, but combining them so the model sees inactivity in context rather than as an absolute number.
- **Month-over-month change in recharge amount, call minutes, data volume, data revenue, and bundle spend** — five "delta" features capturing whether a subscriber's usage is trending down, not just where it currently sits, directly motivated by the behavioral finding that pre-churn usage is a decline, not a flat low level.
- **Age, gender, marital status, and preferred language** — the demographic fields, each shown to have a real, if individually modest, association with churn in the exploratory analysis and the Cramér's V ranking.
- **An explicit flag for whether age was originally missing** — included because missingness itself was found to be non-random (subscribers with missing demographic fields turned out to have systematically different churn risk), so the fact of missingness was kept as information rather than discarded once the value was filled in.

## 4. Feature selection: what was kept, what was dropped, and how

Feature selection ran in two conceptually different stages, both fit only on the training data.

The first stage, **correlation pruning**, looks for pairs of numeric features that move almost in lockstep — if two variables are telling the model nearly the same story, keeping both wastes model capacity and muddies interpretation without adding real signal. Three features were dropped this way, each because it was near-duplicate information already captured by a feature that was kept: a raw recharge-amount total that was perfectly correlated with the recharge-amount average, a voice-revenue average that closely tracked on-net minutes, and a data-package-spend average that closely tracked total data revenue.

The second stage, **importance-based pruning**, works differently: it fits an actual model (a Random Forest) and asks, for each remaining feature, how much predictive accuracy is lost if that feature's values are randomly scrambled. A feature that can be scrambled without hurting predictions isn't carrying real, non-redundant signal. One feature — a count of how many months of usage history a subscriber had — was dropped on this basis, since it turned out to be redundant with tenure and recency information already in the feature set.

Together, these two stages took the feature set from an initial 22 candidate features down to a final **18**, which — together with the subscriber ID, snapshot date, and label — make up the 21-column datasets actually used for modeling.

Missing-value handling for the three demographic categorical fields was deliberately *not* uniform, and the reason ties directly back to the missingness-is-informative finding above. Missing gender and missing marital status were filled in with each field's most common value (fit on the training data only) — a reasonable, neutral default, because neither field's most common category is itself a particularly high- or low-risk group. Preferred language was handled differently: its missing values were kept as their own explicit "Missing" category rather than filled in, because that field's most common value — English — is *also* the single highest-churn language group. Filling missing language with "English" would have quietly assigned those subscribers a specific, elevated risk profile they may not actually share, rather than making a neutral guess. Treating the three fields differently wasn't an inconsistency; it was a direct response to which fields' typical value happened to carry its own risk signal.

## 5. The chain of consequences

This is the part of the project where each step's output didn't just inform the next step — it actively constrained what was possible.

**From multiple snapshots to one, and why that ruled out a chronological split.** A rolling, multi-snapshot design was considered early on, but the raw usage history only spans seven months, and the churn-date discovery (batch-processed, not a precise individual event) made it hard to trust cleanly separated snapshot windows without risking overlap or leakage between them. The project settled on a single fixed snapshot date instead, which simplified the leakage story to one clear boundary — but it also removed the option of a chronological train/test split, since there's no separate future period left to hold out once you've committed to one snapshot. Every subscriber active as of that date has to go into either the training set or the test set, so the split had to be a random, label-stratified one instead of a time-based one.

**Class imbalance, balancing, and the recall/precision trade.** The real-world churn rate in the 90-day prediction window is 8.88%. Training directly on that imbalanced distribution produced a model that essentially refused to predict churn at all — recall near zero — despite a deceptively strong-looking overall ranking score (ROC-AUC around 0.90), because near-perfect accuracy on the majority class is easy to achieve while missing almost every actual churner. Rebalancing the training data to 50/50 (via a categorical-aware variant of SMOTE, since the feature set includes native categorical columns) changed that dramatically: recall jumped to roughly 0.91, at the direct cost of more false positives. That trade was accepted deliberately — for a churn-prevention use case, failing to flag an at-risk customer is normally more costly than an occasional unnecessary retention outreach, so trading some precision for much higher recall was the right call given the goal, not a side effect that went unnoticed.

**Balanced training, real-world testing, and why 0.5 wasn't usable as a cutoff.** Because the models were *fit* on the artificially rebalanced 50/50 data but *evaluated* on the real ~8.88% distribution, their predicted probabilities came out systematically inflated — both tuned models overestimate churn risk at nearly every calibration bin, and the gap gets worse at higher predicted probabilities (LightGBM's Brier score, 0.059, is somewhat better-calibrated than XGBoost's, 0.067, but neither is well-calibrated in an absolute sense). This is precisely why the project couldn't just use the conventional "flag anyone above 50%" rule: a probability of 0.50 out of one of these models doesn't mean the same thing it would from a model trained on real-world proportions. Instead, the optimal decision threshold was found by directly searching for the cutoff that minimizes cost under different assumed ratios of "cost of missing a churner" to "cost of a false alarm." At a 5-to-1 ratio, the resulting thresholds land far below 0.50 — 0.16 for LightGBM, 0.24 for XGBoost — exactly the kind of low, model-specific cutoff you'd expect once the inflated-probability problem is understood.

**Is there overfitting?** This comparison wasn't previously saved, so it was computed fresh for this report: each final tuned model, evaluated on the original training rows it had already seen (at the real, unbalanced churn rate, for a fair comparison against the test set) versus the held-out test rows it had never seen. XGBoost's precision-recall score drops from about 0.66 on training rows to 0.55 on test — an 11-point gap — with recall falling from about 86% to 78%. LightGBM shows a larger gap: precision-recall score falls from about 0.72 on training rows to 0.55 on test, recall from about 81% to 66%. Meanwhile, each model's overall ranking ability (ROC-AUC) barely moves, staying within two to three points of its training-set value for both models. That combination — meaningfully better on rows it has already seen, but no collapse in overall discriminative power on new data — is the signature of moderate, not severe, overfitting, and it's noticeably more pronounced in LightGBM than XGBoost, consistent with LightGBM's tuned configuration (up to 700 trees) giving it more room to fit training-specific patterns. It is not underfitting — training performance is clearly and comfortably ahead of test performance, not equally weak on both — and it's not severe enough to disqualify either model, since test-set ROC-AUC around 0.94 remains strong for both.

**How feature-representation issues changed the explanations, not the model choice.** Two representation problems, if left unfixed, would have quietly distorted which features looked important without changing which model actually performed best. First, keeping both the raw tenure bucket and the continuous tenure-in-days feature would have split one real signal across two overlapping variables, making tenure's true importance look smaller than it is when measured feature-by-feature — this was caught and fixed (the bucket was dropped, and its redundancy with tenure-in-days was later confirmed almost exactly, with only 95 disagreements in ranking out of 393,205 subscribers) before it ever reached modeling. Second, if the categorical demographic fields had been broken into individual one-hot dummy columns the way some earlier importance checks temporarily did for comparison purposes, a single variable's real importance would fragment across its category dummies and look artificially weak. Keeping categories native rather than one-hot-encoded, and dropping the redundant tenure bucket, both changed *explanation clarity* — the resulting SHAP-based importance rankings and dashboard now correctly attribute credit to tenure and to each demographic field as a whole — not which model was ultimately chosen. That choice was made purely on predictive performance (recall, precision, ROC-AUC, PR-AUC), before any of the explanation work happened.

## 6. What ended up mattering most, and what didn't

**Tenure and recent activity mattered the most, consistently, everywhere.** Across the exploratory analysis, the categorical association check, the survival analysis, and the model's own feature-importance rankings, how long someone has been a customer and how recently they've been active came out on top again and again — not just once, by one method, which would be easier to dismiss as a fluke of a particular technique. Core usage levels (recharge amount, data volume, on-net minutes) formed a strong second tier, both in the tree-based importance rankings and in the final model's SHAP-based explanations.

**Gender mattered less than expected, and stayed that way under every lens.** It had the weakest association with churn among the four demographic fields, its survival-analysis significance test came out right at the edge of conventional significance (not quite there), and its effect in the more rigorous Cox model was negligible and not statistically significant either — a rare case where a variable that intuitively "should" matter for a customer-behavior problem simply didn't, once tenure and the other factors were accounted for.

**The clearest genuine surprise was how differently a linear model and a tree-based model disagreed about what mattered.** When Logistic Regression's feature ranking was compared against the tree-based Random Forest ranking, there was essentially no agreement between them — near-zero rank correlation, versus a strong, expected agreement between the Random Forest's two internal importance measures. The two models weren't wrong about each other; they were picking up genuinely different kinds of structure in the same data (raw usage levels and short-term trends for the linear model, tenure and non-linear age effects for the tree model) — a useful reminder that "which features matter" is a model-dependent question, not a fixed property of the dataset.

A second real surprise: missingness itself turned out to be informative rather than just an inconvenience to clean up. Subscribers with missing gender or marital status had systematically *lower* churn risk, confirmed independently in the Cox model — which is exactly why the project treated missingness as a feature in its own right (the age-missing flag) rather than something to quietly fill in and forget.

And a useful negative result: a two-dimensional visual summary of the usage data (PCA) showed churners and non-churners heavily overlapping, with no clean visual separation — confirming that churn isn't a simple "low-usage versus high-usage" split visible along one or two dominant axes, and reinforcing why a flexible, non-linear model was the right tool for this problem rather than a simpler geometric or linear one.
